How few multiplications does a product need?

The rank of a bilinear map is the number of multiplications required to compute it. Strassen doing 2×2 matrix multiplication in seven instead of eight is where fast matrix multiplication comes from, and finding such decompositions in general is open.

Laboratoire Jean Kuntzmann, Université Grenoble Alpes
Supervised by Jean-Guillaume Dumas · 21 May to 15 July 2024
Reimplemented in C++ on Givaro, 2026

What finishing it changed

The internship left one search unfinished: on F3 3×6 its final step never terminated, so the 11 multiplications on record came from an abandoned run. That search now completes in under ten seconds, and reaches 10.

Both columns were measured on one core of an i5-12450H at 2.2 GHz. The difference is the language and what the independence test does, not the hardware.

The finding hiding in the table

Turning the published table into fixtures turned up something its write-up does not draw out. The expensive third step improved the answer in one of four cases. On 4×7 it spent 5 020 of its 5 044 seconds to return the rank the previous step already had; on F3 3×6 it never terminated at all.

So the step that costs essentially everything buys essentially nothing. Making it faster optimises the part that does not pay, which is why the result here came from finishing a run rather than accelerating one.

And now a bound from below

Everything above is a heuristic: it says a map needs no more than so many multiplications. The internship also carried a complete search that asks not “can this be improved” but “is there one with exactly k products”, and a no from it, run to exhaustion, is a fact about the map rather than about the searcher.

The last line is new. The internship could bound 5×5 from above only; its rank is now known to lie between 12 and 14. Deciding 12 is C(961, 3) nodes: about seven hours at the rate the 11 was ruled out at, which is long rather than impossible, and is the next result this work could have.

The second strand has a number now

A fast algorithm costs multiplications and additions both, and the additions are set by how many nonzero entries its operators carry. The internship's write-up on this half reports no measured result, only that the program took too long for a simple problem.

Running the original confirms two of its three defects and corrects the third. Algorithm 2.4 was unreachable, because both arms of the choice that offers it call algorithm 2.3. Its search can return None into a two-variable unpack. But the count it reported was not broken by floating point, because it sends every entry through limit_denominator, which snaps 1.85e-18 to zero. What was broken is the objective inside algorithm 3, which counts zeros with == 0 on raw doubles: on that operator it sees 86 zeros where 144 exist. The count was sound and the quantity being maximised was not.

One pipeline, not two halves

A decomposition is not the deliverable; the algorithm is, and an algorithm is three operators: L and R encoding the two operands, P combining the products. Recovering them was in the original and had been lost, which also meant the two strands here did not know the other existed.

minimise-rank fixtures/f2_5x5.tensor --emit-operators out   # 25 -> 14 multiplications
sparsify-operator out_left.matrix                           # 31 -> 27 nonzeros

Fewer multiplications, then fewer additions, from one map, in two commands.

Where this stops

The heuristic proves nothing optimal: the 10 for F3 3×6 is a better decomposition than the one on record, not a claim about the true rank. The exact search does prove things, but only where it can finish, and from scratch its cost is C(pool, k). Neither settles the bilinear rank problem, which is still open. Nothing in the repository is ever a float, so a reported rank is a fact about the map rather than an artefact of rounding, but exact arithmetic makes an answer trustworthy, not optimal.
